2. OREO Quick Recap (3 mins)

A fast reminder — and a welcome for anyone joining this week. OREO has been the structure behind every lesson this term, spoken and written.

Opinion

"In my view…" — Your position. In writing, this becomes your Topic Sentence.

Reason

"The main reason is…" — Why you believe it.

Example

"For instance…" — A real, specific situation. Then one sentence saying what it shows.

Opinion Restated

"That is why…" — One sentence to close. Your concession from last week lives here.

Full reference: see the OREO & Logic Map sheet.
3 mins For new joiners
"If this is your first class, welcome. OREO — Opinion, Reason, Example, Opinion Restated — is the structure we use for every argument, spoken or written. The full reference is in the sheet."
For returning students
"You know OREO well by now. Today is the last time we review it in Term 1. Keep it in mind while you write — two of its four parts are what we are going to focus on shortly."
⚠ Keep to 3 minutes. Establish it exists, point to the sheet, move on.

4. Key Concept: How the Test Is Timed (3 mins)

In IELTS Writing Task 2 you are given one question and asked to write an essay of at least 250 words, in 40 minutes. This is how that time is normally used:

5 min

Plan
Read the question. Decide your position and your reasons.

30 min

Write
Everything you have learned has to happen here.

5 min

Check
Spelling, articles, verb endings. That is all it is time for.

Nobody rebuilds a paragraph in the last five minutes. Everything you have learned has to go in while you write — and you cannot hold all of it in your head at once.
3 mins Delivery
"One piece of practical information before we finish Term 1. In the real test you get one question, and you write an essay of at least 250 words in forty minutes. Five minutes to plan, thirty to write, five to check. That last five minutes is only enough for spelling, articles, verb endings — nobody rebuilds a paragraph in five minutes. So everything you have learned this term has to go in while you are writing, and you cannot hold all of it in your head at once. Today we look at the two sentences worth protecting above everything else."
Keep it short
⚠ This slide is information, not a technique. Three minutes maximum — the next slide is the useful part.
If a student asks whether they should write more than 250 words: most high scorers write 270–300. Writing much more leaves no time to check.

5. Two Sentences Worth Protecting (7 mins)

Of everything from Term 1, these are the two sentences that do the most to show an examiner you are answering the question and supporting it:

1. The first sentence — Topic Sentence (1-4)

Your position, plus a hint of your reason. Without it, the examiner cannot tell whether you answered the question.

2. The sentence after your example — Explaining Your Evidence (1-8)

What the example shows. Without it, the example is a story, not support.

Both missing

"Schools are important for students. Many students do not know about jobs. My brother did not know what to do after school. So schools should help."

Both in place

Schools should do more to prepare students for work, because most students leave with no idea what any job actually involves. To begin with, the school timetable rarely includes contact with working adults. For instance, my brother finished high school last year without ever having spoken to anyone about their job. This shows that students are being asked to choose a direction with almost no information to base it on.

  Same idea, same example. Two sentences — the first one and the one after the example — carry most of the difference.
7 mins Why these two
"When you are writing against the clock, you cannot think about everything at once. So here are two sentences to protect. The first sentence of your paragraph tells the examiner you are answering the question. The sentence after your example tells them the example is doing a job. Everything else you have learned is useful — but if these two are missing, the rest does not rescue the paragraph."
Working the example (about 4 mins)
Read the left box aloud in a flat voice. Pause. "There is an opinion in there somewhere, and there is a real example. So what is missing?"
Let students answer before you reveal anything. Expected: the first sentence says nothing anyone would disagree with; the brother appears but we are never told what he proves.
Then read the right box and point at the two highlighted sentences. "Same idea. Same brother. The first sentence now states a position and a reason. The last sentence says what the brother proves. Nothing else changed."
Closing
"When you write today, get those two sentences right first. If you have room for hedging, concession and linking words as well, use them — but never at the cost of these two."
⚠ Do not review other skills here. Students already have those phrases from previous lessons — this slide is about what to protect first.

6. Practice 1 — Logic Co-Construction (10 mins)

"Should schools do more to prepare students for work?"
Same prompt as your homework Logic Map. Students with a draft may use it as a starting point.

Mode: Challenger Pair — this is NOT brainstorming

  • Presenter (A) — Share your opinion using your Logic Map. If you did not do the homework, build your map as you speak.
  • Challenger (B) — Your job is not to agree. Ask "Why?", "Can you give a real example?", "What about the other side?" Push until your partner can say their point in one clear sentence.

Steps

  • Round 1 (4 min) — A presents, B challenges. Refine the Logic Map together in the shared doc.
  • Round 2 (4 min) — Switch roles.
  • Return to main room (2 min) — Instructor shows 1–2 Logic Maps.
See next slide for Challenger Questions, Logic Map, and Dialogue example — show this before releasing to breakout rooms.
~1 min Setup — the key instruction
"For the next 10 minutes you are not brainstorming and not agreeing. One of you is Presenter — share your Logic Map. The other is Challenger — make your partner's argument stronger by questioning it. This is shorter than usual because we need time at the end to look back on the term, so keep it moving."
Pairing
Pair students from different countries wherever possible — each side asks questions the other would not. If numbers are uneven, the Assistant joins as Challenger (never Presenter).
About homework
Students who did the homework present their Logic Map. Students who did not can build one while speaking — the Challenger questions guide them.

6. Practice 1 — Challenger Questions & Example

Challenger Question Sequence — ask in this order

  1. "Why do you think so? Is it always true?"
    → fills Reason in the Logic Map
  2. "Can you give me a real example — from your own life?"
    → fills Example
  3. "What is the strongest argument against you?"
    → fills Counter-argument
  4. "Say your position in one sentence — and add why."
    → becomes your Topic Sentence
  Question 4 produces one of today's two protected sentences — get it right here and the paragraph starts well.

Logic Map Template

THESIS: ____________________________ ├─ Reason 1: ______________________ │ └─ Example: ___________________ │ └─ Counter-argument: _______________ └─ My response: ________________

Example Challenger Exchange

A: "My opinion is that schools should do more to prepare students for work."

B (Q1): "Why do you think so? Is it always true?"

A: "Because most students leave school without ever talking to anyone about their job."

B (Q2): "Can you give me a real example from your life?"

A: "My brother finished high school last year, and the only careers class he had was one afternoon."

B (Q3): "What is the strongest argument against you?"

A: "That school time is limited, and adding careers lessons means removing something else."

B (Q4): "Say your position in one sentence — and add why."

A: "Schools should do more to prepare students for work, because most students leave with no idea what any job actually involves."

B: "That is your Topic Sentence. Write it down."

Demo before breakout (~1 min)
Read the dialogue with the Assistant playing A. Emphasize the sequence: "Q1 to Q3 fill the Logic Map. Q4 turns it into the first sentence of the paragraph — the Topic Sentence we just said to protect."
Why a fixed sequence?
The sequence prevents polite agreement and keeps pacing on time. With only 4 minutes per round today, tell pairs to move to Q2 quickly — the example is what makes the paragraph work.
During breakout — what to listen for
✓ Is the Challenger actually challenging, or just agreeing?
✓ At Q4, does the Topic Sentence contain both a position and a reason — or just a position?
⚠ If a pair collapses into agreement, jump in: "B, I haven't heard you push. Ask A — if schools added careers lessons, what would they have to remove?"
After Practice 1 — main room (~2 mins)
"Show 1–2 Topic Sentences that came out of Q4. 'A started with schools should do more. After Q4 it became schools should do more because most students leave with no idea what any job involves. Position plus reason — that is the sentence to protect.'"

7. Practice 2 — Micro-Drafting (8 mins)

Your last paragraph of Term 1. Use your refined Logic Map to write your Introduction + Body 1 in the shared Google Doc. Protect the two sentences from the last slide. Target: about 100 words.

  Brought a paragraph from homework? Paste it in and check those two sentences first. Did not write one? Draft it now from your refined Logic Map — the frame below will help either way.

Paragraph Frame

Introduction — your Topic Sentence:

O "[Subject] may / may not ______, because ______."

Body 1 (4–5 sentences), with an explanation after the example:

R "To begin with, ______. E For instance, ______. This shows that ______. O That is why I would argue that ______."

  Write in the shared doc — in the next activity, a new partner will read your draft.
Introduction + Body 1 only — with one explanation sentence after your Example.
~30 sec Setup
"Now, two groups. If you wrote a paragraph for homework, paste it in and check your first sentence and the sentence after your example. If you did not, draft now from your refined Logic Map. Introduction and Body 1 only, about 100 words, 8 minutes. Protect those two sentences above everything else."
Why two groups
Feedback from a previous run showed many students could not draft from zero within 8 minutes. The optional homework paragraph lets prepared students revise instead of draft. Students without a draft still write from the Logic Map.
Stay in main room during drafting
Students write silently in the shared doc (muted). Keep them in the main room so you can announce "2 minutes left" and make the partner switch fast.
During drafting — what to do
✓ Monitor Google Docs. Do NOT correct.
✓ Check that each student picked one half of the prompt. Trying to cover both produces a vague paragraph.
✓ If finished early: "Go through the checklist. How many of the six can you find in your own paragraph?"
⚠ 8 minutes is the target. If all finish early, move to Peer Review — no need to wait out the clock.

7. Practice 2 — Peer Review with a New Partner (5 mins)

You will be paired with a different partner from Practice 1. Read their draft, then give one strength and one improvement using Point + Problem + Suggestion, as you learned in Lesson 1-10.

  • Does it open with a clear Topic Sentence — and is the question narrowed?
  • Is the Example specific, or just general? ("a friend told me" alone is not specific.)
  • Is there an explanation after the example — does the writer say what it shows?
  • Is there a concession, or at least an acknowledgement of the other side?
  • Is the claim hedged, or too absolute? (always, never, everyone are warning signs.)

Feedback Phrases

Strength — "Your ____ sentence worked well: '____'. It was clear because…"
Suggestion — "One thing that could be stronger is… — maybe you could add…"

Suggested Process

  1. Silent reading (1 min)
    Read your partner's draft. Use the checklist to prepare one strength and one suggestion.
  2. A gives oral feedback (2 min)
    A shares feedback with B. You may also type comments into B's Google Doc.
  3. B gives oral feedback (2 min)
    B shares feedback with A. Same format.
When finished: Return to main room — instructor highlights (2 mins)
~1 min Setup — announce the partner switch
"Drafting time is over, even if you are not finished. Now switch to a new partner — fresh eyes. One minute reading silently: find one sentence that worked and one you had to read twice. Then take turns — 2 minutes each: one strength with the sentence quoted, then Point, Problem, Suggestion. Keep it tight today."
During peer review — what to listen for
✓ Is feedback naming an actual sentence, or staying general? "Which sentence?" is the single most useful thing you can ask a room.
✓ Are students giving specific feedback, or vague praise? Model it if a room is stuck.
After — main room highlights (~2 mins)
"Keep this to one minute today. Quote one piece of feedback that was genuinely usable, then move straight into the reflection — that is the part that matters most in this lesson."
⚠ Watch for students softening feedback into nothing. If a room is only exchanging compliments, join and ask: "Which sentence did you have to read twice?" That question restarts real review.

8. Looking Back on Term 1 (10 mins)

Everyone shares — in the main room

This is the end of Term 1. One by one, everyone says a few words. Choose one of these:

  • Something you can do now that you could not do at the start of this term
  • How your feelings have changed about speaking or writing in English
  • One thing you want to improve in the next term
  Nothing to prepare. A few sentences is enough — it does not need to be perfect.

If you are not sure how to start

  • "At the start of this term, I could not ______. Now I can ______."
  • "Before, speaking in English felt ______. Now it feels ______."
  • "Next term, I want to get better at ______."

Listen to the others — you will find your own words while they speak.

10 mins Setup
"This is the last activity of Term 1, and we stay together in the main room for it. One by one, everyone is going to say a few words about this term. Choose one of the three on screen — something you can do now that you could not do before, how your feelings about English have changed, or one thing you want to improve next term. Nothing to prepare, a few sentences is plenty, and it does not need to be perfect. I will call on you one at a time."
Running it
✓ Do NOT use breakout rooms today. The value is in everyone hearing everyone.
✓ Go around in a fixed order and say so, so nobody is anxious about when their turn comes.
✓ Start with a student likely to speak comfortably — it sets the length for everyone else.
✓ Respond briefly to each one, naming something specific you saw them do this term. Then move on.
⚠ If a student struggles, offer a sentence starter from the right-hand card and wait. Do not skip them — being heard is the point. Halting English is fine here.
⚠ The Assistant and Instructor should each share one too, at the end. Keep both short.
Timing
With 8 students, roughly 45 seconds each plus your short responses fills 10 minutes. If the group is smaller, let them speak longer rather than finishing early.
Closing
"Thank you, everyone. Twelve weeks ago, most of you had never given an opinion in English with a reason and an example attached. Today you did it in writing, under time pressure, and then reviewed someone else's. That is real progress, and it is yours."
