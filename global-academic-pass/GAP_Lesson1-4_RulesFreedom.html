A fast reminder — and a welcome for anyone joining this week. OREO is the hidden structure inside every good paragraph. Today's focus is the opening O.

Opinion

"In my view…" — Your position. In writing, this becomes your Topic Sentence. ← today's focus

Reason

"The main reason is…" — Why you believe it.

Example

"For instance…" — A real, specific situation.

Opinion Restated

"That is why…" — One sentence to close.

4. Key Concept: Writing Clear Topic Sentences (10 mins)

The Topic Sentence is the first sentence of your paragraph. A reader uses it to decide what your whole paragraph is about. A strong one does four things:

  1. It states your position clearly. Not "Rules are important" — everyone agrees with that. Say exactly what you believe.
  2. It hints at your main reason. The reader should sense the direction before Body 1 even begins.
  3. It is one focused sentence. One clear idea — not two or three mixed together.
  4. It answers the exact question. Respond to the specific prompt, not the general topic.
A strong Topic Sentence = your position + a hint of your reason, in one clear sentence.
~3 mins Delivery
"When a reader starts your paragraph, the first sentence tells them everything: what you think, and roughly why. If that sentence is vague, the reader is lost before they begin. So a strong Topic Sentence does four things — a clear position, a hint of the reason, one focused idea, and a direct answer to the question."
Read each point with a quick contrast. E.g. for point 1: "'Rules are important' — is that a position? No, everyone agrees. 'Speed limits mainly protect people' — now that is a position."
Set up the demo
"Let me show you a weak opening, and we will fix it together."

4. Demo — From Weak to Strong

Step 1: Fix the Topic Sentence — add a position and a hint of the reason:

Weak

"Rules are important."

Strong Topic Sentence

"Speed limits on roads mainly protect people, because the small loss of freedom prevents serious accidents."

Step 2: A strong Topic Sentence sets up the whole paragraph:

Topic Sentence + Body 1

OSpeed limits on roads mainly protect people, because the small loss of freedom prevents serious accidents. RThe main reason is that a driver going too fast cannot stop in time to avoid a crash. EFor instance, near my home there is a school zone where cars must slow to 30 km/h; since the limit was introduced, there have been far fewer dangerous moments when children cross the road. OConsequently, although the rule limits how fast people may drive, I would argue that the protection it provides clearly matters more.

  The Topic Sentence O does the heavy lifting. Once it states position + reason, the R, E, and closing O follow naturally.
This covers Introduction + Body 1 only. The Counter-argument is saved for Body 2 — not today.
~4 mins The "aha" moment
Show the Weak → Strong pair first. "Same topic. But now — position: speed limits protect people. Reason hint: they prevent accidents. In one sentence, the reader knows exactly where we are going."
Then reveal the full paragraph. Point at the tags: "The Topic Sentence is the Opinion. Everything after it — Reason, Example, closing Opinion — simply delivers what the first sentence promised."
Key message
"Get the Topic Sentence right, and the paragraph almost writes itself. Get it wrong, and no amount of good writing after it can save the paragraph."
Assistant Real Talk (~2 mins)
"Assistant — when you write, do you find it easier to start once you know your exact point, or do you start writing and figure it out as you go?"
Most people write better once the main point is clear. Reinforce: deciding your position first — the Topic Sentence — makes everything after it easier.
⚠ Do not analyse every sentence. Point at the Topic Sentence and the tags — that is enough.

5. Topic Sentence Toolkit

Three reliable patterns for opening a paragraph with a clear position. Pick whichever fits your argument:

Pattern 1 — State a clear position

"[Subject] should / should not [verb], because [reason]."

e.g., "Cyclists should be required to wear helmets, because they protect riders from serious injury."

Pattern 2 — Acknowledge the other side

"Although [other view], [subject] should [verb]."

e.g., "Although height limits disappoint some children, theme parks need them to keep riders safe."

Pattern 3 — Redefine the idea

"A [X] is not simply [Y]; it is [Z]."

e.g., "A speed limit is not simply a restriction; it is a shared promise to keep each other safe."

  This toolkit is shared in the Zoom chat — keep it open while you write.
~2 mins Delivery
"Three patterns. Pattern 1 is the simplest and works almost every time — position, then 'because', then reason. Pattern 2 shows you see both sides, which sounds mature. Pattern 3 is more advanced — it redefines the idea. Pick the one that fits; you do not need all three."
Read one example per pattern. Do not drill. Students apply them in the drafting phase.
Share the toolkit in chat NOW so it is visible when breakouts open.

6. Practice 1 — Challenger Questions & Example

Challenger Question Sequence — ask in this order

  1. "Why do you think so? Is it always true?"
    → fills Reason in the Logic Map
  2. "Can you give me a real example — from your own life?"
    → fills Example
  3. "What would someone who disagrees say? How would you respond?"
    → fills Counter-argument and Response
  4. "Now — can you say your main point in ONE clear sentence?"
    → becomes the Topic Sentence
  Question 4 is today's key move — it turns the whole discussion into one Topic Sentence.

Logic Map Template

THESIS: ____________________________ ├─ Reason 1: ______________________ │ └─ Example: ___________________ │ └─ Counter-argument: _______________ └─ My response: ________________

Example Challenger Exchange

A: "My opinion is that most rules exist to protect people, not to limit our freedom."

B (Q1): "Why do you think so? Is it always true?"

A: "Because so many everyday rules are about safety — speed limits, helmet rules, standing behind the line on a train platform."

B (Q2): "Can you give me a real example from your life?"

A: "Last year a cyclist near my home crashed, but because a rule made him wear a helmet, he was not badly hurt."

B (Q3): "What would someone who disagrees say?"

A: "Some say helmet rules take away personal choice. But that small loss of freedom is worth avoiding a serious injury."

B (Q4): "Now say your main point in one clear sentence."

A: "Helmet rules for cyclists mainly protect people, because a small loss of freedom prevents serious injury."

B: "That is your Topic Sentence. Write it down."

Demo before breakout (~1 min)
Read the dialogue with the Assistant playing A. Emphasize the sequence: "Q1 to Q3 fill the Logic Map. Then Q4 forces the whole thing into one sentence — that becomes the Topic Sentence they will write."
Why a fixed sequence?
The sequence prevents polite agreement and keeps pacing on time. Q4 is new for this lesson — it converts the discussion into a single Topic Sentence, which is exactly what students need before drafting.
During breakout — what to listen for
✓ Is the Challenger actually challenging, or just agreeing?
✓ At Q4, does the Presenter produce one clear sentence — a real Topic Sentence?
⚠ If a pair collapses into agreement, jump in: "B, I haven't heard you disagree. Ask A — if rules are only about protection, why do some people still find them unfair?"
After Practice 1 — main room (~2 mins)
"Show 1–2 Topic Sentences that came out of Q4. 'A started vague — rules are good. After challenging, it became helmet rules mainly protect people, because a small loss of freedom prevents serious injury. That is a Topic Sentence ready to write.'"

7. Practice 2 — Micro-Drafting (8 mins)

Use your refined Logic Map to write your Introduction + Body 1 in the shared Google Doc — starting with a clear Topic Sentence. Target: about 100 words.

  Brought a paragraph from homework? Paste it in and revise it — check that it opens with a strong Topic Sentence. Did not write one? Draft it now from your refined Logic Map — the frame below will help either way.

Paragraph Frame

Introduction — start with your Topic Sentence:

O "[Subject] should / should not ______, because ______."

Body 1 (4–5 sentences):

R "To begin with, ______. E For instance, ______. O Consequently, ______, and this is why I would argue that ______."

  Write in the shared doc — in the next activity, a new partner will read your draft.
Focus on Introduction + Body 1 only. Do not include the Counter-argument — that belongs in Body 2.
~30 sec Setup
"Now, two groups. If you wrote a paragraph for homework, paste it in and revise it — first check it opens with a strong Topic Sentence. If you did not, draft now from your refined Logic Map. Introduction and Body 1 only, about 100 words, 8 minutes. Use the frame if you need it. Start with your Topic Sentence."
Why two groups
Feedback from a previous run showed many students could not draft from zero within 8 minutes. The optional homework paragraph lets prepared students revise instead of draft. Students without a draft still write from the Logic Map.
Stay in main room during drafting
Students write silently in the shared doc (muted). Keep them in the main room so you can announce "2 minutes left" and make the partner switch fast.
During drafting — what to do
✓ Monitor Google Docs. Do NOT correct.
✓ First check each student's opening sentence — is it a real Topic Sentence, or a vague "Rules are important"? Nudge the vague ones.
✓ If finished early: "Read it silently. Does your first sentence tell a reader your exact position?"
⚠ 8 minutes is the target. If all finish early, move to Peer Review — no need to wait out the clock.

7. Practice 2 — Peer Review with a New Partner (6 mins)

You will now be paired with a different partner from Practice 1. Read your new partner's draft in the shared doc, and prepare oral feedback using the checklist below. See the suggested process on the right.

  • Does it open with a clear Topic Sentence — a position plus a hint of the reason?
  • Does Body 1 contain the Reason, Example, and a closing Opinion?
  • Is the Example specific, or just general? ("my friend" alone is not specific.)
  • Are there linking words? (To begin with, For instance, Consequently…)
  • Does the closing sentence add something, not just repeat the Topic Sentence?

Feedback Phrases

Positive — "Your Topic Sentence was clear because…"
Suggestion — "One thing that could be stronger is… — maybe you could add…"

Suggested Process

  1. Silent reading (2 min)
    Read your partner's draft. Use the checklist to prepare one positive comment and one suggestion.
  2. A gives oral feedback (2 min)
    A shares feedback with B. You may also type comments into B's Google Doc.
  3. B gives oral feedback (2 min)
    B shares feedback with A. Same format.
When finished: Return to main room — instructor highlights (2 mins)
~1 min Setup — announce the partner switch
"Drafting time is over, even if you are not finished. Now switch to a new partner — fresh eyes. Move to your breakout room. Spend 2 minutes reading your partner's draft silently and preparing feedback. Then take turns — 2 minutes each. You can also type comments into their Google Doc."
During peer review — what to listen for
✓ Is the first checklist item — the Topic Sentence — actually being checked? That is today's focus.
✓ Are students giving specific feedback, or vague praise? Model it if a room is stuck.
After — main room highlights (~2 mins)
"Quote one strong Topic Sentence you saw. Then one common improvement (anonymous): 'A few openings were still general — "rules matter." Next time, name your exact position and one reason in that first sentence.'"
⚠ Do not correct grammar now. Today is Topic Sentences, logic, and cohesion only.
