2. OREO Quick Recap (3 mins)

A fast reminder — and a welcome for anyone joining this week. Today we focus especially on how we word the O and the R.

Opinion

"In my view…" — What you believe. ← today's focus

Reason

"The main reason is…" — Why you believe it.

Example

"For instance…" — A real situation that supports it.

Opinion Restated

"That is why…" — One sentence to close.

Full reference: see the OREO & Logic Map sheet.

3. Warm-up (5 mins)

Quick Choice & Why

Choose one and answer in 30 seconds:

A. "What is one thing you always do before an important test?"

B. "Where do you study best — at home, at school, or somewhere else? Why?"

C. "Have you ever changed the way you study? What made you change it?"

5 mins Setup
"Let's warm up. Three questions on the screen — choose the one that feels most natural, and take about 30 seconds. I will call on each of you. No right or wrong answer — just speak."
Calling on students

4. Key Concept: Hedging Language (10 mins)

Strong claims are easy to attack — one exception destroys them. Hedging means softening a statement so it stays true in most cases, and sounds more thoughtful. Four simple tools:

1. Softening verbs

tends to / seems to / appears to

2. Modal verbs

may / might / can / could

3. Limiting the scope

in many cases / for most students / generally

4. Distancing phrases

It could be argued that... / Research suggests that...

Demo

Instructor & Assistant: "Absolute claim" vs. "Hedged claim"

"Exams are useless. Students always forget everything after the test."

→ How can we soften this?

Note: Use one of the four tools — verb, modal, scope, or distancing.
10 mins Explaining the skill
"Listen to this: 'Exams are useless. Students always forget everything.' Is that true? Always? Every student? One counter-example and the whole argument collapses. Hedging protects your point. Instead of 'always,' say 'tend to.' Instead of 'useless,' say 'may not measure long-term learning very well.' You still make your point — but now it is defensible."
Demo — Absolute version first
"Watch. [Assistant], ask me: 'Do you think exams are a good way to measure learning?'"
Assistant asks. You reply: "Exams are useless. Students always forget everything after the test." — Then pause. "So — is that convincing, or does it sound too extreme?"
Let students answer. Guide them toward the absolute words — useless, always, everything. Then move to the next slide for the hedged version.

4. Key Concept: From Absolute to Hedged

Absolute

"Exams are useless. Students always forget everything after the test."

useless / always / everything — one exception and the whole argument collapses.

Hedged

"Exams may not measure long-term learning very well. In many cases, students tend to forget details soon after the test, although the habit of revising can still be valuable."

  • Modal: may not — instead of "are"
  • Scope: in many cases — instead of "always"
  • Softening verb: tend to — instead of "do"
  • Balance: although... can still be valuable
  Same opinion — but now it is careful, credible, and much harder to disagree with.
Delivering the hedged version
"Now watch the same point, softened." — Read the Hedged version naturally, then point to the four tags. "May not. In many cases. Tend to. Nothing extreme — and yet the opinion is still perfectly clear."
Closing
"Hedging is not weakness — it is precision. You do not need all four tools at once. Aim for at least one hedge in every opinion you give today."
⚠ Do not ask students to practise here. Practice happens in Sessions 1 and 2. Keep this to explanation and demo only.

5. Academic Vocabulary

Small changes make a big difference in how academic you sound:

Instead of "good"

  • Effective — "An effective way to..." "Testing yourself is an effective way to check what you really remember."
  • Valuable — "...is valuable when..." "Feedback from a teacher is valuable when you are preparing for an exam."
  • Beneficial — "It is beneficial to..." "Reviewing a little every day is beneficial because it strengthens memory."

Instead of "bad"

  • Inefficient — "It is inefficient to..." "Reading the same page again and again is inefficient."
  • Stressful — "...can be stressful." "Studying only the night before an exam can be stressful."
  • Challenging — "It can be challenging to..." "It can be challenging to study alone without a clear plan."

Instead of "I think":  "To my mind..."  /  "As I see it..."

~4 mins Introduction
"One more tool before the discussions: Academic Vocabulary. The words you choose signal how carefully you are thinking. Two of these — beneficial and challenging — you met in Lesson 1-1, so this is a quick refresher. The other four are new, and they fit today's topic of learning well."
Walking through the vocabulary
"Instead of 'good,' try 'effective' — testing yourself is an effective way to study. Or 'valuable' — feedback is valuable when you are preparing. Instead of 'bad,' try 'inefficient' — reading the same page again and again is inefficient. Or 'stressful.' And instead of 'I think,' try 'To my mind' or 'As I see it.'"
Read two or three aloud — you do not need all of them. Students pick up the rest through use in Sessions 1 and 2.
Transition into Session 1
"In Session 2, your challenge will be to use at least two of these words while still hedging your claims. For now, let's move into the first discussion."

6. 1-on-1 Exchange — Session 1 (15 mins)

PROMPT

"How do you prepare for an important exam? Describe exactly what you do."

Steps

  • A: Share opinion using OREO
  • B: Ask one follow-up question
  • A: Respond → "How about you?"
  • B: Share opinion using OREO
  • A: Ask one follow-up question
  • Open discussion — agree, disagree, cultural differences
  • Tell your partner one thing they did well

Follow-up question ideas

  • "How long have you studied that way?"
  • "Does that method always work for you?"
  • "What would you do differently with more time?"
  • "Is exam preparation different in your country?"
Today's focus: Soften your claims — use tends to, may, or in many cases instead of always and never.
When finished: Return to main room — instructor highlights 1-2 good hedges (1-2 mins)
15 mins Setup
"Into breakout rooms. Today's first prompt is on the screen: how you prepare for an important exam — describe exactly what you do. Person A shares using OREO, and try to soften any strong claims. Person B listens, then asks one follow-up question. Then A says 'How about you?' and B goes. Discuss freely with the time left, and before you return, tell your partner one thing they did well. I'll move between rooms."
Pairing
Pair students from different countries where possible — exam systems and study culture differ a lot, and the contrast is the value. If numbers are uneven, the Assistant or Instructor steps in to keep pairs 1-on-1.
During breakout — what to listen for
✓ Listen for absolute words — always, never, everyone, all. Note one to soften afterwards.
✓ Note 1-2 natural hedges you hear — you will highlight these after.
⚠ If a pair is stuck, prompt: "Tell me one thing you do the night before — then say whether it works every single time."
After Session 1 — feedback (1-2 mins)
"Welcome back. I heard a nice hedge: [quote it]. Notice it did not weaken the opinion — it made it more believable. Let's carry that into Session 2."
One or two observations only. Save detailed feedback for after Session 2.

7. 1-on-1 Exchange — Session 2 (15 mins)

PROMPT

"Which helps you learn more — studying alone, or studying with other people? Why?"

Steps — same format as Session 1

  • A: Share opinion using OREO
  • B: Ask one follow-up question
  • A: Respond → "How about you?"
  • B: Share opinion using OREO
  • A: Ask one follow-up question
  • Open discussion — agree, disagree, cultural differences
  • Tell your partner one thing they did well

Session 2 Challenge

Use at least two of these words in your OREO response:

Effective — "An effective way to..."
Valuable — "...is valuable when..."
Beneficial — "It is beneficial to..."
Inefficient — "It is inefficient to..."
Stressful — "...can be stressful."
To my mind... — instead of "I think"
Today's focus: Keep softening your claims — tends to, may, in many cases — even when you feel strongly.
When finished: Return to main room — instructor feedback & group discussion (3 mins)
15 mins Setup
"Same steps, new prompt, one challenge. This time, try to use at least two of the Academic Vocabulary words on the right in your OREO response — do not force them, find a natural moment. And keep hedging, as we practised. Into breakout rooms."
During breakout — what to listen for
✓ Are students attempting the Academic Vocabulary? Even one word used naturally is worth highlighting.
✓ Are students still hedging, as in Session 1? Note the strongest example for feedback.
⚠ If a student makes an absolute claim, ask one pointed question: "Is that true for every student, every time?"
After Session 2 — group feedback (3 mins)
"Good work. First, a strong moment: [positive observation — e.g., 'someone said studying alone tends to work better for them, rather than always works better — small word, big difference.']. Second, one thing to keep working on: [common issue — e.g., 'several of you still said everyone. Try most students instead.']."
Experience corner — asking the Assistant / Instructor
"Assistant / Instructor — when you were a student, was there a study method you were completely sure about that turned out not to work? What changed your mind?"
Keep it personal and reflective. This models why hedging matters — being certain about learning methods is often exactly where people turn out to be wrong.

Logic Map for Next Week

Next week's class is Logic & Writing — we will turn a spoken argument about learning into a written paragraph. To prepare, fill in the Logic Map Reference Sheet (shared as a PDF) for the prompt below.

PROMPT

"Do exam scores really measure how much a person has learned?"

WHAT TO DO (required)
Complete the four parts of the map: Thesis, one Reason, one Example (keep it specific), and one Counter-argument with your response. Keep each line short and specific.
IF POSSIBLE (optional)
If you have time, turn your Logic Map into a short paragraph (about 80–100 words) using OREO. This step is optional — next week's class works well either way.

  Bring your completed map to next week's class — and your short paragraph too, if you wrote one.

~1 min Delivery
"Your homework for next week. Next class is Logic & Writing, where we turn today's thinking into written paragraphs. I've shared the Logic Map Reference Sheet as a PDF. On page 2, fill in a Logic Map for the prompt on screen — whether exam scores really measure how much a person has learned. Completing the four parts is required. If you have extra time, you may also turn it into a short paragraph using OREO — that part is optional. Bring whatever you have."
Why the optional paragraph
Attempting a short paragraph at home — optionally — means next week's Writing class can focus on revising with a partner's feedback rather than drafting from zero. The Logic Map alone remains fully sufficient; the paragraph is a bonus for students who have time.
Connecting to today's skill
Remind them: the Thesis line is the best place to practise today's skill. "Exam scores may not fully measure learning" is far stronger than "Exam scores are useless" — and it survives next week's Challenger questions.
