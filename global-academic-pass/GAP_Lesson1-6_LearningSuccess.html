2. OREO Quick Recap (3 mins)

A fast reminder — and a welcome for anyone joining this week. OREO is the hidden structure inside every good paragraph. Today's focus is the closing O, where we answer the other side.

Opinion

"In my view…" — Your position. In writing, this becomes your Topic Sentence.

Reason

"The main reason is…" — Why you believe it.

Example

"For instance…" — A real, specific situation.

Opinion Restated

"That is why…" — One sentence to close. This is where your concession lives. ← today's focus

Full reference: see the OREO & Logic Map sheet.
3 mins For new joiners
"If this is your first class, welcome. OREO — Opinion, Reason, Example, Opinion Restated — is the structure we use for every argument, spoken or written. The full reference is in the sheet."
For returning students
"You know OREO well. Here is today's focus: the closing O. Instead of simply repeating your opinion, you first admit what is true about the other side, and then restate your point. That move is called concession, and it is what today is about."

4. Key Concept: Concession Structure (10 mins)

A concession means admitting that part of the other side is true — and then explaining why your position still holds. It makes your argument sound fair and confident rather than one-sided. A strong concession does four things:

  1. It names the other side accurately. Use their strongest argument, not a weak version of it.
  2. It admits what is genuinely true. "It is true that…" / "Admittedly…"
  3. It turns back with a clear signal. "However," / "Nevertheless," / "That said,"
  4. It ends on your position. The last idea the reader hears should be yours.
A strong concession = admit what is true + turn back to your point, in one or two sentences.
~3 mins Delivery
"If you only ever say why you are right, you sound one-sided. But if you admit what is true about the other view first, and then explain why your point still stands, you sound fair and confident. That move is called concession, and it is one of the clearest signs of mature academic writing."
Read each point with a quick contrast. E.g. for point 1: "'Some people say exams are useful, but they are wrong' — is that naming the other side accurately? No, it dismisses them. 'It is true that exams are objective and easy to compare' — now the reader trusts you."
Set up the demo
"Let me show you a weak attempt at this, and we will fix it together."

4. Demo — From Weak to Strong

Step 1: Fix the concession — admit what is true, then turn back:

Weak

"Some people say exams are useful, but they are wrong."

Strong Concession

"It is true that exams provide a fair, measurable standard for comparing students. However, a single score tends to capture what a learner remembered on one morning rather than what they truly understand."

Step 2: The concession closes the paragraph and answers the other side:

Introduction + Body 1, closing with a concession

OExam scores may not fully measure how much a person has learned, because they capture one moment rather than long-term understanding. RThe main reason is that most exams reward short-term memory more than deep understanding. EFor instance, a classmate of mine memorised fifty history dates the night before a test, scored well, and could not recall any of them a month later. OIt is true that exams provide a clear and fair standard for comparison. Nevertheless, I would argue that a score alone gives an incomplete picture of what a student has actually learned.

  The concession sits in the closing O. It does not weaken your argument — it shows you considered the other side, and then closes on your point.
Today the counter-argument appears as one concession sentence inside Body 1. A full Body 2 comes in a later lesson.
~4 mins The "aha" moment
Show the Weak → Strong pair first. "Same position. But now the other side is named accurately — exams are fair and measurable — and admitted as true. Then 'However' turns it back. The reader feels heard, and still ends on our point."
Then reveal the full paragraph. Point at the tags: "Opinion, Reason, Example — and then the closing Opinion is where the concession lives. Two sentences: admit, then turn back."
Key message
"A concession is not a retreat. Admitting one true thing about the other side makes everything else you say more believable."
Assistant Real Talk (~2 mins)
"Assistant — when you disagree with someone, do you find it easier to persuade them by arguing harder, or by first agreeing with something they said?"
Most people find that agreeing with something first lowers resistance. Reinforce: concession is persuasive, not polite decoration.
⚠ Do not analyse every sentence. Point at the closing two sentences and the tags — that is enough.

5. Concession Toolkit

Three reliable patterns for admitting the other side and turning back to your point. Pick whichever fits your argument:

Pattern 1 — Admit, then turn back

"It is true that [other side]. However, [your point]."

e.g., "It is true that exams are easy to compare fairly. However, they show only part of what a student can do."

Pattern 2 — Concede inside one sentence

"Although [other side], [your point]."

e.g., "Although exams are objective, they rarely measure creativity or independent thinking."

Pattern 3 — Accept the value, limit the scope

"[Other side] is useful for [X], but not for [Y]."

e.g., "Testing is useful for checking facts, but not for showing whether a student truly understands them."

  This toolkit is shared in the Zoom chat — keep it open while you write.
~2 mins Delivery
"Three patterns. Pattern 1 is the simplest and works almost every time — admit, then 'However'. Pattern 2 packs it into one sentence with 'Although'. Pattern 3 is more precise — it accepts the value of the other side but limits where it applies. Pick the one that fits; you do not need all three."
Read one example per pattern. Do not drill. Students apply them in the drafting phase.
Share the toolkit in chat NOW so it is visible when breakouts open.

6. Practice 1 — Challenger Questions & Example

Challenger Question Sequence — ask in this order

  1. "Why do you think so? Is it always true?"
    → fills Reason in the Logic Map
  2. "Can you give me a real example — from your own life?"
    → fills Example
  3. "What is the strongest argument against you?"
    → fills Counter-argument
  4. "Now — admit their point, and still keep yours, in one sentence."
    → becomes your Concession
  Question 4 is today's key move — it turns the counter-argument into a concession sentence.

Logic Map Template

THESIS: ____________________________ ├─ Reason 1: ______________________ │ └─ Example: ___________________ │ └─ Counter-argument: _______________ └─ My response: ________________

Example Challenger Exchange

A: "My opinion is that exam scores do not fully measure what a student has learned."

B (Q1): "Why do you think so? Is it always true?"

A: "Because most exams reward what you happen to remember on one particular day."

B (Q2): "Can you give me a real example from your life?"

A: "Last year I memorised fifty history dates the night before a test. I scored well, but a month later I could not recall any of them."

B (Q3): "What is the strongest argument against you?"

A: "That exams are objective — everyone takes the same test under the same conditions."

B (Q4): "Now admit their point, and still keep yours, in one sentence."

A: "It is true that exams are objective and easy to compare. Nevertheless, a single score tends to show what a student remembered that morning, not what they understand."

B: "That is your concession. Write it down."

Demo before breakout (~1 min)
Read the dialogue with the Assistant playing A. Emphasize the sequence: "Q1 to Q3 fill the Logic Map. Then Q4 forces the counter-argument into a concession — that is the sentence that will close their paragraph."
Why a fixed sequence?
The sequence prevents polite agreement and keeps pacing on time. Q3 and Q4 are sharpened for this lesson — Q3 asks for the strongest objection, and Q4 converts it into a concession, which is exactly what students need before drafting.
During breakout — what to listen for
✓ Is the Challenger actually challenging, or just agreeing?
✓ At Q4, does the Presenter actually admit something true — or do they just say the other side is wrong?
⚠ If a pair collapses into agreement, jump in: "B, I haven't heard you push. Ask A — if scores mean so little, why does almost every school and university still use them?"
After Practice 1 — main room (~2 mins)
"Show 1–2 concessions that came out of Q4. 'A started with — exams are useless. After challenging, it became it is true that exams are objective and easy to compare; nevertheless, a score shows what was remembered that morning. That is a concession ready to write.'"

7. Practice 2 — Micro-Drafting (8 mins)

Use your refined Logic Map to write your Introduction + Body 1 in the shared Google Doc — finishing with a concession sentence. Target: about 100 words.

  Brought a paragraph from homework? Paste it in and revise it — add a concession at the end. Did not write one? Draft it now from your refined Logic Map — the frame below will help either way.

Paragraph Frame

Introduction — your Topic Sentence:

O "[Subject] may / may not ______, because ______."

Body 1 (4–5 sentences), closing with a concession:

R "To begin with, ______. E For instance, ______. O It is true that ______. However, I would argue that ______."

  Write in the shared doc — in the next activity, a new partner will read your draft.
Introduction + Body 1 only — closing with one concession sentence. A full Body 2 comes in a later lesson.
~30 sec Setup
"Now, two groups. If you wrote a paragraph for homework, paste it in and revise it — then add a concession at the end. If you did not, draft now from your refined Logic Map. Introduction and Body 1 only, about 100 words, 8 minutes. Use the frame if you need it. Finish with: It is true that... However..."
Why two groups
Feedback from a previous run showed many students could not draft from zero within 8 minutes. The optional homework paragraph lets prepared students revise instead of draft. Students without a draft still write from the Logic Map.
Stay in main room during drafting
Students write silently in the shared doc (muted). Keep them in the main room so you can announce "2 minutes left" and make the partner switch fast.
During drafting — what to do
✓ Monitor Google Docs. Do NOT correct.
✓ First check each student's closing sentences — do they admit something genuinely true, or just dismiss the other side? Nudge the dismissive ones.
✓ If finished early: "Read it silently. Would someone who disagrees with you feel that you understood their point?"
⚠ 8 minutes is the target. If all finish early, move to Peer Review — no need to wait out the clock.

7. Practice 2 — Peer Review with a New Partner (6 mins)

You will now be paired with a different partner from Practice 1. Read your new partner's draft in the shared doc, and prepare oral feedback using the checklist below. See the suggested process on the right.

  • Is there a concession — does the writer admit something genuinely true about the other side?
  • Does it open with a clear Topic Sentence — a position plus a hint of the reason?
  • Is the claim hedged, or too absolute? (always, never, everyone are warning signs.)
  • Is the Example specific, or just general? ("my friend" alone is not specific.)
  • Are there linking words? (To begin with, For instance, However…)

Feedback Phrases

Positive — "Your concession worked well because…"
Suggestion — "One thing that could be stronger is… — maybe you could add…"

Suggested Process

  1. Silent reading (2 min)
    Read your partner's draft. Use the checklist to prepare one positive comment and one suggestion.
  2. A gives oral feedback (2 min)
    A shares feedback with B. You may also type comments into B's Google Doc.
  3. B gives oral feedback (2 min)
    B shares feedback with A. Same format.
When finished: Return to main room — instructor highlights (2 mins)
~1 min Setup — announce the partner switch
"Drafting time is over, even if you are not finished. Now switch to a new partner — fresh eyes. Move to your breakout room. Spend 2 minutes reading your partner's draft silently and preparing feedback. Then take turns — 2 minutes each. You can also type comments into their Google Doc."
During peer review — what to listen for
✓ Is the first checklist item — the concession — actually being checked? That is today's focus.
✓ Are students giving specific feedback, or vague praise? Model it if a room is stuck.
After — main room highlights (~2 mins)
"Quote one strong concession you saw. Then one common improvement (anonymous): 'A few paragraphs said the other side is simply wrong. Next time, name one thing that is genuinely true about their view before you answer it.'"
⚠ Do not correct grammar now. Today is concession, logic, and cohesion only.
