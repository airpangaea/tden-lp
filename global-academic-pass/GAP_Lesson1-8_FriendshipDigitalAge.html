2. OREO Quick Recap (3 mins)

A fast reminder — and a welcome for anyone joining this week. OREO is the hidden structure inside every good paragraph. Today's focus is the sentence that comes right after the E.

Opinion

"In my view…" — Your position. In writing, this becomes your Topic Sentence.

Reason

"The main reason is…" — Why you believe it.

Example

"For instance…" — A real, specific situation. Then one sentence saying what it shows. ← today's focus

Opinion Restated

"That is why…" — One sentence to close. Your concession from last week lives here.

Full reference: see the OREO & Logic Map sheet.
3 mins For new joiners
"If this is your first class, welcome. OREO — Opinion, Reason, Example, Opinion Restated — is the structure we use for every argument, spoken or written. The full reference is in the sheet."
For returning students
"You know OREO well. Here is today's focus: the gap between the E and the closing O. Most people give an example and move straight on, assuming the reader sees the point. They do not. Today we add the sentence that makes the example count."
⚠ Keep to 3 minutes. Establish it exists, point to the sheet, move on.

4. Key Concept: Explaining Your Evidence (10 mins)

Most writers give an example and stop, assuming the reader will see the point. They will not. A strong paragraph adds one sentence after the example that says what it shows. A good explanation does four things:

  1. It says what the example shows. "This shows that…" / "This suggests that…"
  2. It connects back to your Reason. Not a new idea — the same one, now proved.
  3. It generalises carefully. Use hedging: "This suggests that, for many students…"
  4. It stays to one sentence. An explanation, not a second example.
A strong explanation = what the example shows + how it supports your reason, in one sentence.
~3 mins Delivery
"Here is the most common weakness I see in student writing. A student gives a good, specific example — and then moves straight on. But an example is not an argument. The reader is left thinking: so what? One sentence fixes it. You say what the example shows, and suddenly the example is doing work."
Read each point with a quick contrast. E.g. for point 1: "'Almost nobody replied. So social media is bad.' — is that an explanation? No, it just repeats the opinion. 'This shows that a shared online space does not automatically create conversation' — now the example has meaning."
Set up the demo
"Let me show you a paragraph with this problem, and we will fix it together."

4. Demo — From Weak to Strong

Step 1: Replace the empty last sentence with a real explanation:

Weak

"...almost nobody replied. So social media is bad."

Strong Explanation

"...almost nobody replied. This shows that a shared online space does not automatically create conversation — being contactable is not the same as being close."

Step 2: The explanation sits between the Example and the closing Opinion:

Introduction + Body 1, with the explanation in place

OSocial media may be pushing people apart rather than bringing them closer, because it replaces real conversation with easy contact. RThe main reason is that reacting to a post takes far less effort than talking, so people often choose the easier option. EFor instance, last year my class created a group chat of thirty-eight students, and after the first week almost nobody replied to anything. This shows that a shared online space does not automatically create conversation — being contactable is not the same as being close. OIt is true that social media keeps distant friends within reach; nevertheless, I would argue that easy contact has quietly replaced the conversations that build friendship.

  The highlighted sentence is the new move. Without it, the example is just a story; with it, the example becomes evidence.
Introduction + Body 1 only, as before — with one explanation sentence added after your Example.
~4 mins The "aha" moment
Show the Weak → Strong pair first. "Same example, same opinion. But 'so social media is bad' told us nothing. 'This shows that a shared online space does not automatically create conversation' tells us exactly what those thirty-eight silent students prove."
Then reveal the full paragraph. Point at the highlighted sentence: "Opinion, Reason, Example — then the explanation — then the closing Opinion with last week's concession still in it. Every sentence is doing a different job."
Key message
"An example is not an argument. The sentence after it is where the thinking shows."
Assistant Real Talk (~2 mins)
"Assistant — when you tell someone a story to make a point, do you explain the point afterwards, or do you assume they got it?"
Most people assume the point is obvious — and it usually is not. Reinforce: in writing, you never get to check whether the reader understood, so you say it.
⚠ Do not analyse every sentence. Point at the highlighted explanation sentence — that is enough.

5. Explanation Toolkit

Three reliable patterns for the sentence that comes after your example. Pick whichever fits:

Pattern 1 — Say what it shows

"This shows that [X]."

e.g., "This shows that a shared online space does not automatically create conversation."

Pattern 2 — Generalise carefully

"This suggests that [X], at least for [group]."

e.g., "This suggests that, at least for my generation, contact and closeness are not the same thing."

Pattern 3 — Correct the expectation

"The point is not that [Y], but that [X]."

e.g., "The point is not that we stopped talking, but that talking became optional."

  This toolkit is shared in the Zoom chat — keep it open while you write.
~2 mins Delivery
"Three patterns. Pattern 1 is the simplest and works almost every time — 'This shows that…'. Pattern 2 adds hedging, which you learned two weeks ago, so your generalisation stays defensible. Pattern 3 is sharper — it corrects what the reader might have assumed. Pick the one that fits; you do not need all three."
Read one example per pattern. Do not drill. Students apply them in the drafting phase.
Share the toolkit in chat NOW so it is visible when breakouts open.

6. Practice 1 — Challenger Questions & Example

Challenger Question Sequence — ask in this order

  1. "Why do you think so? Is it always true?"
    → fills Reason in the Logic Map
  2. "Can you give me a real example — from your own life?"
    → fills Example
  3. "What is the strongest argument against you?"
    → fills Counter-argument
  4. "Now — what does your example actually show? One sentence."
    → becomes your Explanation
  Question 4 is today's key move — it turns a story into evidence.

Logic Map Template

THESIS: ____________________________ ├─ Reason 1: ______________________ │ └─ Example: ___________________ │ └─ Counter-argument: _______________ └─ My response: ________________

Example Challenger Exchange

A: "My opinion is that social media is pushing people apart rather than bringing them closer."

B (Q1): "Why do you think so? Is it always true?"

A: "Because reacting to a post takes almost no effort, so people do that instead of talking."

B (Q2): "Can you give me a real example from your life?"

A: "Last year my class made a group chat with thirty-eight people. After the first week, almost nobody replied to anything."

B (Q3): "What is the strongest argument against you?"

A: "That social media keeps distant friends within reach — I still see what my old classmates are doing."

B (Q4): "Now — what does your example actually show? One sentence."

A: "It shows that a shared online space does not automatically create conversation; being contactable is not the same as being close."

B: "That is your explanation. Write it down."

Demo before breakout (~1 min)
Read the dialogue with the Assistant playing A. Emphasize the sequence: "Q1 to Q3 fill the Logic Map. Then Q4 asks what the example proves — and that is the sentence most students never write."
Why a fixed sequence?
The sequence prevents polite agreement and keeps pacing on time. Q4 is changed for this lesson — instead of producing a concession, it produces the explanation sentence. Students who did last week's lesson still have their concession for the closing sentence.
During breakout — what to listen for
✓ Is the Challenger actually challenging, or just agreeing?
✓ At Q4, does the Presenter interpret the example — or just repeat their opinion louder?
⚠ If a pair collapses into agreement, jump in: "B, I haven't heard you push. Ask A — if social media pushes people apart, why do so many people say it is how they keep old friends?"
After Practice 1 — main room (~2 mins)
"Show 1–2 explanations that came out of Q4. 'A had a good example — thirty-eight students, nobody replied. After Q4 it became: this shows a shared online space does not automatically create conversation. That is an explanation ready to write.'"

7. Practice 2 — Micro-Drafting (8 mins)

Use your refined Logic Map to write your Introduction + Body 1 in the shared Google Doc — including an explanation sentence after your Example. Target: about 100 words.

  Brought a paragraph from homework? Paste it in and revise it — add an explanation sentence after your example. Did not write one? Draft it now from your refined Logic Map — the frame below will help either way.

Paragraph Frame

Introduction — your Topic Sentence:

O "[Subject] may / may not ______, because ______."

Body 1 (4–5 sentences), with an explanation after the example:

R "To begin with, ______. E For instance, ______. This shows that ______. O That is why I would argue that ______."

  Write in the shared doc — in the next activity, a new partner will read your draft.
Introduction + Body 1 only — with one explanation sentence after your Example.
~30 sec Setup
"Now, two groups. If you wrote a paragraph for homework, paste it in and revise it — then add an explanation after your example. If you did not, draft now from your refined Logic Map. Introduction and Body 1 only, about 100 words, 8 minutes. Use the frame if you need it. The key sentence starts: This shows that..."
Why two groups
Feedback from a previous run showed many students could not draft from zero within 8 minutes. The optional homework paragraph lets prepared students revise instead of draft. Students without a draft still write from the Logic Map.
Stay in main room during drafting
Students write silently in the shared doc (muted). Keep them in the main room so you can announce "2 minutes left" and make the partner switch fast.
During drafting — what to do
✓ Monitor Google Docs. Do NOT correct.
✓ First check: after each example, is there a sentence saying what it shows? That is today's focus. Nudge anyone who jumps straight from example to conclusion.
✓ If finished early: "Read it silently. If you deleted your example, would the paragraph lose anything? If not, your explanation is not doing its job."
⚠ 8 minutes is the target. If all finish early, move to Peer Review — no need to wait out the clock.

7. Practice 2 — Peer Review with a New Partner (6 mins)

You will now be paired with a different partner from Practice 1. Read your new partner's draft in the shared doc, and prepare oral feedback using the checklist below. See the suggested process on the right.

  • Is there an explanation after the example — does the writer say what it shows?
  • Is there a concession, or at least an acknowledgement of the other side?
  • Does it open with a clear Topic Sentence — a position plus a hint of the reason?
  • Is the claim hedged, or too absolute? (always, never, everyone are warning signs.)
  • Is the Example specific, or just general? ("my friend" alone is not specific.)

Feedback Phrases

Positive — "Your explanation made the example count because…"
Suggestion — "One thing that could be stronger is… — maybe you could add…"

Suggested Process

  1. Silent reading (2 min)
    Read your partner's draft. Use the checklist to prepare one positive comment and one suggestion.
  2. A gives oral feedback (2 min)
    A shares feedback with B. You may also type comments into B's Google Doc.
  3. B gives oral feedback (2 min)
    B shares feedback with A. Same format.
When finished: Return to main room — instructor highlights (2 mins)
~1 min Setup — announce the partner switch
"Drafting time is over, even if you are not finished. Now switch to a new partner — fresh eyes. Move to your breakout room. Spend 2 minutes reading your partner's draft silently and preparing feedback. Then take turns — 2 minutes each. You can also type comments into their Google Doc."
During peer review — what to listen for
✓ Is the first checklist item — the explanation — actually being checked? That is today's focus.
✓ Are students giving specific feedback, or vague praise? Model it if a room is stuck.
After — main room highlights (~2 mins)
"Quote one strong explanation you saw. Then one common improvement (anonymous): 'A few paragraphs still jumped from the example straight to the conclusion. Next time, add one sentence: this shows that…'"
⚠ Do not correct grammar now. Today is explanation, logic, and cohesion only.
