2. OREO Quick Recap (3 mins)

A fast reminder — and a welcome for anyone joining this week. Today we focus on what happens before your OREO, in the seconds after you hear the question.

Opinion

"In my view…" — What you believe. Today, one sentence comes before this. ← today's focus

Reason

"The main reason is…" — Why you believe it.

Example

"For instance…" — A real situation that supports it.

Opinion Restated

"That is why…" — One sentence to close.

4. Key Concept: Paraphrasing the Question (10 mins)

Big questions are usually too broad to answer well. Paraphrasing means saying the question back in your own words, smaller, before you answer it. Four moves, in order:

1. Say it back

"So the question is whether..." / "If I understand correctly, you are asking..."

2. Make it smaller

"I will focus on [one part]..." — choose one angle you can actually support.

3. Check the meaning

"By [word], I mean..." — define the vague word before you use it.

4. Then answer it

Give your OREO — but answer the smaller question, not the huge one.

Demo

Instructor & Assistant: "Drifting" vs. "Narrowing"

Q: "In a world full of information, how can we tell what is true?"
A: "Information is very important. There is a lot of information now. We should be careful."

→ Did that answer the question?

Note: Use the four moves in order — say it back, make it smaller, check the meaning, then answer.
10 mins Explaining the skill
"Two weeks ago we practised buying a moment when someone challenges you. Today is different — this is about accuracy, not composure. A question like 'how can we tell what is true?' is too big to answer directly, so people drift into vague sentences. The fix is to shrink it first. Say the question back in your own words. Choose one part you can actually support. Define any vague word. Then give your OREO on that smaller question. You will sound focused instead of lost."
Demo — the drifting version first
"Watch. [Assistant], ask me: 'In a world full of information, how can we tell what is true?'"
Assistant asks. You reply vaguely: "Information is very important. There is a lot of information now. We should be careful." — Then pause. "So — did I answer the question, or did I just talk about the topic?"
Let students answer. Guide them toward what is missing — no position, nothing specific, and the question itself was never narrowed. Then move to the next slide for the narrowed version.

4. Key Concept: From Drifting to Narrowing

Drifting

"Information is very important. There is a lot of information now. We should be careful."

True, but it answers nothing. The question was never narrowed, so there is nothing specific to say.

Narrowing

"So the question is whether ordinary people can check information themselves. I will focus on what students like me can realistically do. By 'true', I mean something you can trace back to its original source. In my view, checking where a claim came from is the most practical test we have..."

  • 1. Say it back: "So the question is whether..."
  • 2. Make it smaller: "I will focus on what students can do."
  • 3. Check the meaning: "By 'true', I mean..."
  • 4. Then answer: OREO on the smaller question
  Same question — but now it is small enough to answer with something real.
Delivering the narrowed version
"Now watch the same question, narrowed first." — Deliver it at a natural speed, then point to the four tags. "Notice I did not answer the huge question. I made it smaller, said what I meant by 'true', and then answered the version I could actually support."
Closing
"Narrowing a question is not avoiding it. Nobody can answer 'how do we know what is true' in thirty seconds — but everybody can answer a smaller version of it. Today, before every answer, use at least the first two moves."
⚠ Do not ask students to practise here. Practice happens in Sessions 1 and 2. Keep this to explanation and demo only.

5. Academic Vocabulary

Small changes make a big difference in how academic you sound:

Instead of "good"

  • Reliable — "a reliable source" "A school website is usually a reliable source for school events."
  • Credible — "...sounds credible." "A report is more credible when it names the people involved."
  • Valuable — "...is valuable when..." "A second source is valuable when a story sounds surprising."

Instead of "bad"

  • Misleading — "...can be misleading." "A headline can be misleading even when the article is accurate."
  • Biased — "...may be biased." "A review written by the seller may be biased."
  • Challenging — "It can be challenging to..." "It can be challenging to find where a claim originally came from."

Instead of "I think":  "From what I have seen..."  /  "As far as I can tell..."

~4 mins Introduction
"One more tool before the discussions: Academic Vocabulary. Two of these — valuable and challenging — you met in Lesson 1-5, so this is a quick refresher. The other four are new, and they are the words people actually use when talking about information."
Walking through the vocabulary
"Instead of 'good,' try 'reliable' — a school website is a reliable source for school events. Or 'credible' — a report is more credible when it names people. Instead of 'bad,' try 'misleading' — a headline can be misleading even when the article is accurate. Or 'biased.' And instead of 'I think,' try 'From what I have seen' or 'As far as I can tell.'"
Read two or three aloud — you do not need all of them. Students pick up the rest through use in Sessions 1 and 2.
Transition into Session 1
"In Session 2, your challenge will be to use at least two of these words while still narrowing the question first. For now, let's move into the first discussion."

6. 1-on-1 Exchange — Session 1 (15 mins)

PROMPT

"Where do you usually find out about things — school news, world events, or things you are interested in? Do you believe what you see?"

Steps

  • A: Share opinion using OREO
  • B: Ask one follow-up question
  • A: Respond → "How about you?"
  • B: Share opinion using OREO
  • A: Ask one follow-up question
  • Open discussion — agree, disagree, cultural differences
  • Tell your partner one thing they did well

Follow-up question ideas

  • "How did you first come across it?"
  • "Would you check it anywhere else?"
  • "What would make you doubt it?"
  • "Is that where people in your country usually look?"
Today's focus: When you answer the prompt — say the question back in your own words and make it smaller. Follow-up questions are already small, so answer those directly.
When finished: Return to main room — instructor highlights 1-2 good paraphrases (1-2 mins)
15 mins Setup
"Into breakout rooms. Today's first prompt is on the screen. Before you answer it, do today's new move: say the question back in your own words, and choose one part to focus on. Then give your OREO on that smaller version. Person B listens, asks one follow-up question, then it is your turn — and you paraphrase first too. Before you return, tell your partner one thing they did well. I'll move between rooms."
Pairing
Pair students from different countries where possible — where people look for information differs a lot, and the contrast is the value. If numbers are uneven, the Assistant or Instructor steps in to keep pairs 1-on-1.
During breakout — what to listen for
✓ Does the speaker paraphrase at all, or jump straight into answering? Jumping in is the main failure mode.
✓ Is the paraphrase actually smaller than the original, or just the same question repeated?
⚠ If a student says "I do not really follow the news," redirect: "That is fine — where do you hear about things at school, or about something you like?"
After Session 1 — feedback (1-2 mins)
"Welcome back. I heard a good paraphrase: [quote it]. Notice how much easier the answer became once the question got smaller. Let's carry that into Session 2."
One or two observations only. Save detailed feedback for after Session 2.

Session 2 Challenge

Use at least two of these words in your OREO response:

Reliable — "a reliable source"
Credible — "...sounds credible."
Valuable — "...is valuable when..."
Misleading — "...can be misleading."
Biased — "...may be biased."

Today's focus: When you answer the prompt — keep paraphrasing first: say it back, then make it smaller.
When finished: Return to main room — instructor feedback & group discussion (3 mins)
15 mins Setup
"Same steps, new prompt, one challenge. This time, try to use at least two of the Academic Vocabulary words on the right in your OREO response — do not force them, find a natural moment. And keep paraphrasing before you answer. Into breakout rooms."
During breakout — what to listen for
✓ Are students attempting the Academic Vocabulary? Even one word used naturally is worth highlighting.
✓ Are students still paraphrasing, or skipping straight to the answer now that the prompt feels easier?
⚠ If a student skips it, model it once: "Try starting with — so the question is what I would do before believing it."
After Session 2 — group feedback (3 mins)
"Good work. First, a strong moment: [positive observation — e.g., 'someone said by surprising, I mean something I would want to check — defining the word made the whole answer sharper.']. Second, one thing to keep working on: [common issue — e.g., 'several paraphrases were just the question repeated. Make it smaller, not the same size.']."
Experience corner — asking the Assistant / Instructor
"Assistant / Instructor — at university, how do you check whether a source is worth using? Has anything ever turned out to be wrong after you had already used it?"
Keep it practical. This is one of the clearest links between today's lesson and real university work, so let the Assistant speak concretely about what checking a source actually looks like.

Task 2 — Main Room (3 mins)

Share one reflection with everyone:

  • "Today I found it difficult to..."
  • "One paraphrasing phrase I want to use more is..."
  • "Something my partner said that surprised me was..."
8 mins Setup
"Last activity. Japanese students in Room 1, Indonesian students in Room 2. In your own language, discuss: which was harder today — saying the question back in your own words, or deciding which smaller part to answer? Why? The Assistant joins Room 1, the Instructor joins Room 2 to give one piece of advice each. Five minutes, then we share."
Brief for the Assistant / Instructor
Listen for what students find hard, and offer one practical tip each. For example: "If choosing a part is hard, pick the one where you already have a real example. That is always the right part to answer."
Task 2 — Main room (3 mins)
"Welcome back. Can one person from each group share — what was harder, and what advice did you get?"
Hear from 2-3 students. Do not go around the whole group — time is short.

Complete the four parts of the map: Thesis, one Reason, one Example (keep it specific), and one Counter-argument with your response. Keep each line short and specific.

Attempting a short paragraph at home — optionally — means next week's Writing class can focus on revising with a partner's feedback rather than drafting from zero. The Logic Map alone remains fully sufficient; the paragraph is a bonus for students who have time.
Connecting to today's skill
Remind them to use today's skill on the Thesis line: the prompt is deliberately huge, so narrow it first. "We can never be certain, but checking the original source is the most practical test" is far more workable than trying to answer the whole question.
