2. OREO Quick Recap (3 mins)

A fast reminder — and a welcome for anyone joining this week. OREO is the hidden structure inside every good paragraph. Today you will use it twice: to write, and to check a partner's writing.

Opinion

"In my view…" — Your position. In writing, this becomes your Topic Sentence.

Reason

"The main reason is…" — Why you believe it.

Example

"For instance…" — A real, specific situation. Then one sentence saying what it shows.

Opinion Restated

"That is why…" — One sentence to close. Your concession from last week lives here.

Full reference: see the OREO & Logic Map sheet.
3 mins For new joiners
"If this is your first class, welcome. OREO — Opinion, Reason, Example, Opinion Restated — is the structure we use for every argument, spoken or written. The full reference is in the sheet."
For returning students
"You know OREO well. Today OREO becomes your checklist. When you read a partner's paragraph, you are looking for these four parts — and the fastest way to give useful feedback is to name which one is missing or weak."
⚠ Keep to 3 minutes. Establish it exists, point to the sheet, move on.

4. Key Concept: Peer Review Skills (10 mins)

You have given feedback in every writing lesson so far. Most of it sounded kind but changed nothing. Usable feedback follows a three-step shape — and one rule about how to say it:

  1. 1. Point — name the sentence. "In your third sentence…" Not "your paragraph".
  2. 2. Problem — say what is unclear. "…I could not tell whose opinion this was."
  3. 3. Suggestion — offer one fix. "You could add who said it." One, not five.
  4. The rule: comment on the writing, not the writer. "This sentence is unclear," never "you are unclear."
Usable feedback = Point + Problem + Suggestion. If the writer cannot act on it, it is not feedback.
~3 mins Delivery
"You have all given feedback before, and most of it was polite and useless. 'It was good, I liked it.' The writer smiles and changes nothing. Usable feedback has three parts. Point at one sentence. Say what was unclear about it. Offer one thing to try. And one rule above all: you comment on the sentence, never on the person. That is what makes it safe to be direct."
Read each point with a quick contrast. E.g. for step 1: "'Your paragraph was a bit confusing' — which part? The writer cannot use that. 'In your third sentence, I could not tell whose opinion it was' — now they know exactly where to look."
Set up the demo
"Let me show you some real feedback, and we will fix it together."

Here is feedback one student gave another. Read it and think: what could the writer actually do with this?

Feedback that changes nothing

Discussion: What is missing?

Which sentence is being discussed? What exactly was the problem? What should the writer change? And what is the second sentence doing at all?

4. Demo — From Weak to Strong

Step 1: Rebuild the comment using Point + Problem + Suggestion:

Weak

"Maybe you could add a bit more detail somewhere."

Strong Feedback

"In your example sentence, you say a friend sent you something false. I could not tell where it came from or how you found out — so the example did not fully convince me. You could add where you checked it."

Step 2: Complete feedback on one paragraph — one strength, one improvement:

One strength, then one improvement

Strength — "Your opening sentence worked well: checking the original source is the most practical test we have. I knew your position immediately, and it was narrow enough to defend."

Improvement — "In your example sentence, you say a friend sent you something false. I could not tell where it came from or how you found out, so the example did not fully convince me. You could add where you checked it."

  Point, Problem, Suggestion — highlighted above. Notice every comment is about a sentence, never about the person.
The writing task is unchanged: Introduction + Body 1. What changes today is how you review it.
~4 mins The "aha" moment
Show the Weak → Strong pair first. "'Add a bit more detail somewhere' — where? 'In your example sentence, I could not tell where it came from — you could add where you checked it.' Now the writer knows the sentence, the problem, and one thing to try."
Then reveal the full feedback. "One strength, one improvement. The strength quotes the actual sentence, so it is real praise, not politeness. The improvement follows Point, Problem, Suggestion. That is the whole technique."
Key message
"If your partner cannot act on what you said, you have not given feedback — you have given a compliment."
Assistant Real Talk (~2 mins)
"Assistant — at university, do classmates comment on each other's work? How direct is it, and did that feel uncomfortable at first?"
This is one of the most valuable moments in Term 1. Critiquing a classmate's work is routine at university and uncomfortable for many students at first. Let the Assistant say plainly that it felt strange and then became normal — that is exactly the barrier this course exists to lower.
⚠ Do not lecture on politeness. The point is not softening — it is being specific enough to be useful.

5. Feedback Toolkit

Sentence starters for each step. Use one from each row:

Naming a strength — quote it

"Your [opening / example] sentence worked well: '____'. It was clear because ____."

Quote the actual words. Praise without a quotation is politeness, not feedback.

Point + Problem

"In your ____ sentence, I could not tell ____." / "I had to read ____ twice, because ____."

Describe your experience as a reader — it is honest and never sounds like an attack.

Suggestion — offer one

"You could try ____." / "One option would be to ____."

One suggestion, not five. The writer decides whether to take it.

  This toolkit is shared in the Zoom chat — keep it open while you write.
~2 mins Delivery
"Three rows, one from each. Notice the middle row: 'I could not tell…', 'I had to read it twice…'. You are describing what happened to you as a reader. That is honest, it is specific, and it never sounds like an attack — which is why it works even between students who have only just met."
Read one example per pattern. Do not drill. Students apply them in the drafting phase.
Share the toolkit in chat NOW so it is visible when breakouts open.

6. Practice 1 — Logic Co-Construction (15 mins)

"In a world full of information, how can we tell what is true?"
Same prompt as your homework Logic Map. Students with a draft may use it as a starting point.

Mode: Challenger Pair — this is NOT brainstorming

  • Presenter (A) — Share your opinion using your Logic Map. If you did not do the homework, build your map as you speak.
  • Challenger (B) — Your job is not to agree. Ask "Why?", "Can you give a real example?", "What about the other side?" Push until your partner can say their point in one clear sentence.

Steps

  • Round 1 (6 min) — A presents, B challenges. Refine the Logic Map together in the shared doc.
  • Round 2 (6 min) — Switch roles.
  • Return to main room (2 min) — Instructor shows 1–2 Logic Maps.
See next slide for Challenger Questions, Logic Map, and Dialogue example — show this before releasing to breakout rooms.
~1 min Setup — the key instruction
"For the next 15 minutes you are not brainstorming and not agreeing. One of you is Presenter — share your Logic Map. The other is Challenger — make your partner's argument stronger by questioning it, until they can state their point in one clear sentence. A friendly sparring match."
Pairing
Pair students from different countries wherever possible — each side asks questions the other would not. If numbers are uneven, the Assistant joins as Challenger (never Presenter).
About homework
Students who did the homework present their Logic Map. Students who did not can build one while speaking — the Challenger questions guide them.

6. Practice 1 — Challenger Questions & Example

Challenger Question Sequence — ask in this order

  1. "Why do you think so? Is it always true?"
    → fills Reason in the Logic Map
  2. "Can you give me a real example — from your own life?"
    → fills Example
  3. "What is the strongest argument against you?"
    → fills Counter-argument
  4. "This question is huge — which part are you actually answering?"
    → sharpens your Thesis
  Question 4 uses last week's skill — narrow the question before you commit to a Thesis.

Logic Map Template

THESIS: ____________________________ ├─ Reason 1: ______________________ │ └─ Example: ___________________ │ └─ Counter-argument: _______________ └─ My response: ________________

Example Challenger Exchange

A: "My opinion is that we can never be completely certain, but checking the original source is the most practical test."

B (Q1): "Why do you think so? Is it always true?"

A: "Because most false stories fall apart as soon as you ask where they first came from."

B (Q2): "Can you give me a real example from your life?"

A: "Last month a friend sent me a post saying our school would close early. Nobody could say where it came from, and it was not true."

B (Q3): "What is the strongest argument against you?"

A: "That most people have no time to check sources, so it is not realistic advice."

B (Q4): "This question is huge — which part are you actually answering?"

A: "Not how anyone can know the truth — just what a student can realistically do in a few minutes."

B: "Good. Put that in your Thesis before you write."

Demo before breakout (~1 min)
Read the dialogue with the Assistant playing A. Emphasize the sequence: "Q1 to Q3 fill the Logic Map. Q4 is last week's skill applied to writing — this prompt is enormous, so decide which smaller version you are answering before you write a word."
Why a fixed sequence?
The sequence prevents polite agreement and keeps pacing on time. Q4 changes for this lesson because the prompt is unusually broad — students who narrow it here write far better paragraphs than those who try to answer it whole.
During breakout — what to listen for
✓ Is the Challenger actually challenging, or just agreeing?
✓ At Q4, does the Presenter actually narrow the question — or restate the same huge version?
⚠ If a pair collapses into agreement, jump in: "B, I haven't heard you push. Ask A — if checking sources is so easy, why do false stories spread so fast?"
After Practice 1 — main room (~2 mins)
"Show 1–2 narrowed Theses that came out of Q4. 'A started with how can anyone know the truth. After Q4 it became what a student can realistically check in a few minutes. That is a Thesis you can actually write about.'"

7. Practice 2 — Micro-Drafting (8 mins)

Use your refined Logic Map to write your Introduction + Body 1 in the shared Google Doc. Everything you have learned so far applies. Target: about 100 words.

  Brought a paragraph from homework? Paste it in and revise it using your narrowed Thesis. Did not write one? Draft it now from your refined Logic Map — the frame below will help either way.

Paragraph Frame

Introduction — your Topic Sentence:

O "[Subject] may / may not ______, because ______."

Body 1 (4–5 sentences), with an explanation after the example:

R "To begin with, ______. E For instance, ______. This shows that ______. O That is why I would argue that ______."

  Write in the shared doc — in the next activity, a new partner will read your draft.
Introduction + Body 1 only — with one explanation sentence after your Example.
~30 sec Setup
"Now, two groups. If you wrote a paragraph for homework, paste it in and revise it — start from the narrowed Thesis you just built. If you did not, draft now from your refined Logic Map. Introduction and Body 1 only, about 100 words, 8 minutes. Write carefully — in a few minutes someone will read this closely."
Why two groups
Feedback from a previous run showed many students could not draft from zero within 8 minutes. The optional homework paragraph lets prepared students revise instead of draft. Students without a draft still write from the Logic Map.
Stay in main room during drafting
Students write silently in the shared doc (muted). Keep them in the main room so you can announce "2 minutes left" and make the partner switch fast.
During drafting — what to do
✓ Monitor Google Docs. Do NOT correct.
✓ Check that Theses are narrowed. A paragraph answering the whole prompt will be vague, and vague writing is hard to review — which weakens today's main activity.
✓ If finished early: "Read it as if you were the reviewer. Which sentence would you point at first?"
⚠ 8 minutes is the target. If all finish early, move to Peer Review — no need to wait out the clock.

7. Practice 2 — Peer Review with a New Partner (6 mins)

This is today's main activity. You will be paired with a different partner from Practice 1. Read their draft, then give one strength and one improvement using Point + Problem + Suggestion.

  • Does it open with a clear Topic Sentence — and is the question narrowed?
  • Is the Example specific, or just general? ("a friend told me" alone is not specific.)
  • Is there an explanation after the example — does the writer say what it shows?
  • Is there a concession, or at least an acknowledgement of the other side?
  • Is the claim hedged, or too absolute? (always, never, everyone are warning signs.)

Feedback Phrases

Strength — "Your ____ sentence worked well: '____'. It was clear because…"
Suggestion — "One thing that could be stronger is… — maybe you could add…"

Suggested Process

  1. Silent reading (2 min)
    Read your partner's draft. Use the checklist to prepare one positive comment and one suggestion.
  2. A gives oral feedback (2 min)
    A shares feedback with B. You may also type comments into B's Google Doc.
  3. B gives oral feedback (2 min)
    B shares feedback with A. Same format.
When finished: Return to main room — instructor highlights (2 mins)
~1 min Setup — announce the partner switch
"Drafting time is over, even if you are not finished. Now switch to a new partner — fresh eyes. Two minutes reading silently. As you read, find one sentence that worked and one you had to read twice. Then take turns — 2 minutes each: one strength with the sentence quoted, then Point, Problem, Suggestion. You can also type comments into their Google Doc."
During peer review — what to listen for
✓ Is feedback naming an actual sentence, or staying general? "Which sentence?" is the single most useful thing you can ask a room.
✓ Are students giving specific feedback, or vague praise? Model it if a room is stuck.
After — main room highlights (~2 mins)
"Quote one piece of feedback that was genuinely usable — say why it worked. Then one common improvement (anonymous): 'Several comments were still about the paragraph as a whole. Point at one sentence; that is what makes it fixable.'"
⚠ Watch for students softening feedback into nothing. If a room is only exchanging compliments, join and ask: "Which sentence did you have to read twice?" That question restarts real review.
