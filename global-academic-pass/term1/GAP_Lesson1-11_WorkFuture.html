2. OREO Quick Recap (3 mins)

A fast reminder — and a welcome for anyone joining this week. Today we focus on making the shape of your OREO audible, so a listener can follow it.

Opinion

"In my view…" — What you believe.

Reason

"The main reason is…" — Why you believe it. Today you announce these before you give them. ← today's focus

Example

"For instance…" — A real situation that supports it.

Opinion Restated

"That is why…" — One sentence to close.

Full reference: see the OREO & Logic Map sheet.

4. Key Concept: Signposting (10 mins)

A reader can go back and re-read. A listener cannot. Signposting means saying out loud where your answer is going, so nobody gets lost. Four moves, in order:

1. Announce how many

"I have two reasons for this." — the listener now knows what to wait for.

2. Mark each point

"Firstly..." / "Secondly..." / "More importantly..."

3. Signal a turn

"On the other hand..." / "That said..." — warn them the direction is changing.

4. Mark the ending

"To sum up..." / "So overall..." — so they know you are finishing.

Demo

Instructor & Assistant: "Unmarked" vs. "Signposted"

Q: "Should high school students be allowed to work part-time?"
A: "I think it is fine because you learn about money and my cousin did it but also school is busy and some students get tired and grades can drop so it depends really."

→ How many points was that? Where did each one start?

Note: Use the four moves in order — announce, mark, turn, close.
10 mins Explaining the skill
"Here is the problem with longer answers. Everything is clear in your head — but the listener only hears one continuous stream of words, and they cannot go back. Signposting fixes that. First, announce how many points you have, so they know what to expect. Then mark each one — firstly, secondly. Warn them when you change direction — on the other hand. And tell them when you are finishing — to sum up. Four small phrases, and suddenly a long answer is easy to follow."
Demo — the unmarked version first
"Watch. [Assistant], ask me: 'Should high school students be allowed to work part-time?'"
Assistant asks. Reply in one long breathless stream: "I think it is fine because you learn about money and my cousin did it but also school is busy and some students get tired and grades can drop so it depends really." — Then pause. "So — how many points did I make? Could you say where each one started?"
Let students answer — they will disagree about how many points there were, which is exactly the point. The content was fine; only the shape was missing. Then move to the next slide for the signposted version.

4. Key Concept: From Unmarked to Signposted

Unmarked

"I think it is fine because you learn about money and my cousin did it but also school is busy and some students get tired and grades can drop so it depends really."

The ideas are good. But nothing tells the listener how many points there are, or when one ends and the next begins.

Signposted

"In my view, students should be allowed to work part-time. I have two reasons. Firstly, handling your own money teaches something school cannot teach. Secondly, my cousin worked in a café and became far more confident with strangers. On the other hand, students who work late do sometimes fall behind. So overall, I would allow it, but with a limit on the hours."

  • 1. Announce how many: "I have two reasons."
  • 2. Mark each point: "Firstly... Secondly..."
  • 3. Signal a turn: "On the other hand..."
  • 4. Mark the ending: "So overall..."
  Same ideas, same length — but now the listener can follow every turn.
Delivering the signposted version
"Now watch the same ideas, signposted." — Deliver it at a natural speed, pausing slightly at each marker, then point to the four tags. "Notice I did not add a single new idea. Same content, same length — I only told you where I was going."
Closing
"Signposting is not decoration. It is how you keep a listener with you when your answer is longer than a sentence. Today, every time you speak, announce how many points you have before you give them."
⚠ Do not ask students to practise here. Practice happens in Sessions 1 and 2. Say once that this is a Speaking skill — a reader can re-read, a listener cannot — then move on.

5. Academic Vocabulary

Small changes make a big difference in how academic you sound:

Instead of "good"

  • Rewarding — "...is rewarding because..." "Teaching looks rewarding because you see people change."
  • Stable — "a stable..." "My parents value a stable income more than an exciting job."
  • Meaningful — "meaningful work" "For me, meaningful work matters more than a famous company."

Instead of "bad"

  • Demanding — "...is demanding." "Medical training is demanding, and it lasts many years."
  • Unpredictable — "...is unpredictable." "The job market is unpredictable, so flexible skills help."
  • Excessive — "...are excessive." "In some companies the working hours are excessive."

Instead of "I think":  "I imagine that..."  /  "What appeals to me is..."

~4 mins Introduction
"One more tool before the discussions: Academic Vocabulary. Two of these — meaningful and excessive — you met in Lesson 1-7, so this is a quick refresher. The other four are new, and they are the words people use when talking about work."
Walking through the vocabulary
"Instead of 'good,' try 'rewarding' — teaching looks rewarding because you see people change. Or 'stable' — my parents value a stable income. Instead of 'bad,' try 'demanding' — medical training is demanding. Or 'unpredictable' — the job market is unpredictable. And instead of 'I think,' try 'I imagine that' or 'What appeals to me is.'"
Read two or three aloud — you do not need all of them. Students pick up the rest through use in Sessions 1 and 2.
Transition into Session 1
"In Session 2, your challenge will be to use at least two of these words while still signposting your answer. For now, let's move into the first discussion."

6. 1-on-1 Exchange — Session 1 (15 mins)

PROMPT

"Should high school students be allowed to work part-time? Why or why not?"

Steps

  • A: Share opinion using OREO
  • B: Ask one follow-up question
  • A: Respond → "How about you?"
  • B: Share opinion using OREO
  • A: Ask one follow-up question
  • Open discussion — agree, disagree, cultural differences
  • Tell your partner one thing they did well

Follow-up question ideas

  • "Is that allowed at your school?"
  • "Do you know anyone who does it?"
  • "What would change if there were no limit?"
  • "Would your family see it the same way?"
Today's focus: Announce how many points you have before you give them, then mark each one — firstly, secondly, so overall.
When finished: Return to main room — instructor highlights 1-2 well-signposted answers (1-2 mins)
15 mins Setup
"Into breakout rooms. Today's first prompt is on the screen. As you answer, do today's new move: before you give your reasons, say how many you have — 'I have two reasons' — then mark them as you go. Person B listens, asks one follow-up question, then it is your turn. Before you return, tell your partner one thing they did well. I'll move between rooms."
Pairing
Pair students from different countries where possible — school rules and family attitudes about student work differ a lot, and the contrast is the value. If numbers are uneven, the Assistant or Instructor steps in to keep pairs 1-on-1.
During breakout — what to listen for
✓ Does the speaker announce how many points before giving them? Skipping that is the main failure mode.
✓ Do the markers match the content — does "secondly" actually introduce a second reason, or repeat the first?
⚠ Nobody needs personal experience of a part-time job to answer this. If a student hesitates, prompt: "You do not need to have done it — just say whether you think it should be allowed, and why."
After Session 1 — feedback (1-2 mins)
"Welcome back. I heard a well-signposted answer: [quote it]. Notice you could follow it from start to finish without effort. Let's carry that into Session 2."
One or two observations only. Save detailed feedback for after Session 2.

7. 1-on-1 Exchange — Session 2 (15 mins)

PROMPT

"What makes people satisfied with their work — the money, the hours, or the work itself?"

Steps — same format as Session 1

  • A: Share opinion using OREO
  • B: Ask one follow-up question
  • A: Respond → "How about you?"
  • B: Share opinion using OREO
  • A: Ask one follow-up question
  • Open discussion — agree, disagree, cultural differences
  • Tell your partner one thing they did well

Session 2 Challenge

Use at least two of these words in your OREO response:

Rewarding — "...is rewarding because..."
Stable — "a stable..."
Meaningful — "meaningful work"
Demanding — "...is demanding."
Unpredictable — "...is unpredictable."
I imagine that... — instead of "I think"
Today's focus: Keep signposting — this prompt offers three options, so announce which ones you are taking before you start.
When finished: Return to main room — instructor feedback & group discussion (3 mins)
15 mins Setup
"Same steps, new prompt, one challenge. This time, try to use at least two of the Academic Vocabulary words on the right in your OREO response — do not force them, find a natural moment. And keep signposting: there are three things to choose from here, so tell your partner which ones you are taking before you start. Think about adults you have actually seen at work. Into breakout rooms."
During breakout — what to listen for
✓ Are students attempting the Academic Vocabulary? Even one word used naturally is worth highlighting.
✓ Are students still signposting, or dropping the markers once they get involved in the topic?
⚠ If a student is stuck, point them at someone they have observed: "Think of one adult you know who seems happy at work, and one who does not. What is different?"
After Session 2 — group feedback (3 mins)
"Good work. First, a strong moment: [positive observation — e.g., 'someone announced I have two reasons and then actually gave exactly two — easy to follow from beginning to end.']. Second, one thing to keep working on: [common issue — e.g., 'a few of you said firstly and then never said secondly. If you announce two, deliver two.']."
Experience corner — asking the Assistant / Instructor
"Assistant / Instructor — did you have a part-time job as a student? What did it teach you that school did not?"
Keep this concrete — one job, one thing learned. If you did not have one, say what you observed in a family member instead. Avoid turning it into advice about their futures.

In your own language, discuss: which was harder today — deciding how many points you had, or keeping track of them while speaking? Why? Brief for the Assistant / Instructor

"Welcome back. Can one person from each group share — what was harder, and what advice did you get?"
Hear from 2-3 students. Do not go around the whole group — time is short.
