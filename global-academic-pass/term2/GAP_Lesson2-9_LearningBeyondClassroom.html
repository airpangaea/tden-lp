2. OREO Quick Recap (3 mins)

A fast reminder — and a welcome for anyone joining this week. Today we focus on what happens before your OREO, in the seconds after you hear the question.

Opinion

"In my view…" — What you believe. Today, one sentence comes before this. ← today's focus

Reason

"The main reason is…" — Why you believe it.

Example

"For instance…" — A real situation that supports it.

Opinion Restated

"That is why…" — One sentence to close.

Full reference: see the OREO & Logic Map sheet.
3 mins For new joiners
"If this is your first Global Academic Pass class, welcome. OREO is the structure we use every time we give an opinion: Opinion, Reason, Example, Opinion Restated. The full reference is in the sheet — keep it open in another tab. You will pick it up quickly today."
For returning students
"If you were here last week, you know OREO well by now. Here is today's focus: the moment before you start. A question like 'what is the best way for young people to learn?' is enormous. If you answer it as it is, you drift. Today we learn to say the question back in our own words, shrink it, and then answer the smaller version."

4. Key Concept: Paraphrasing the Question (10 mins)

Big questions are usually too broad to answer well. Paraphrasing means saying the question back in your own words, smaller, before you answer it. Four moves, in order:

1. Say it back — in new words

"So the question is how / whether..." / "In other words, ..." — change the key words.

2. Make it smaller

"I will focus on [one part]..." — choose one angle you can actually support.

3. Check the meaning

"By [word], I mean..." — define the vague word before you use it.

4. Then answer it

Give your OREO — but answer the smaller question, not the huge one.

Demo

Instructor & Assistant: "Drifting" vs. "Narrowing"

Q: "What is the best way for young people to learn?"
A: "Learning is very important. There are many ways to learn. Everyone is different."

→ Did that answer the question?

Note: Use the four moves in order — say it back in new words, make it smaller, check the meaning, then answer.
10 mins Explaining the skill
"Two weeks ago we practised buying a moment when someone challenges you. Saying the question back also gives you a few seconds to think — but its main job is accuracy: you answer what was actually asked. A question like 'what is the best way for young people to learn?' is too big to answer directly, so people drift into vague sentences. The fix is to shrink it first. Say the question back — and change the key words, do not just read it out again. Choose one part you can actually support. Define any vague word. Then give your OREO on that smaller question."
Demo — the drifting version first
"Watch. [Assistant], ask me: 'What is the best way for young people to learn?'"
Assistant asks. You reply vaguely: "Learning is very important. There are many ways to learn. Everyone is different." — Then pause. "So — did I answer the question, or did I just talk about the topic?"
Let students answer. Guide them toward what is missing — no position, nothing specific, and the question itself was never narrowed. Then move to the next slide for the narrowed version.

4. Key Concept: From Drifting to Narrowing

Drifting

"Learning is very important. There are many ways to learn. Everyone is different."

True, but it answers nothing. The question was never narrowed, so there is nothing specific to say.

Narrowing

"So the question is how teenagers learn most effectively. I will focus on practical skills, like cooking or riding a bike. By 'effectively', I mean in a way you still remember months later. In my view, doing it yourself, with someone nearby to help, works better than only watching..."

  • 1. Say it back: "young people" → "teenagers", "the best way" → "most effectively"
  • 2. Make it smaller: "I will focus on practical skills."
  • 3. Check the meaning: "By 'effectively', I mean..."
  • 4. Then answer: OREO on the smaller question
  Same question — but now it is in your own words, and small enough to answer with something real.
Delivering the narrowed version
"Now watch the same question, narrowed first." — Deliver it at a natural speed, then point to the four tags. "Notice I did not answer the huge question. I made it smaller, said what I meant by 'effectively', and then answered the version I could actually support."
Point out the first tag: "young people" became "teenagers", and "the best way" became "most effectively". Reading the question back word for word is the most common mistake — changed words show you understood it.
Closing
"Narrowing a question is not avoiding it. Nobody can answer 'what is the best way to learn' in thirty seconds — but everybody can answer a smaller version of it. Today, before you answer each prompt, use at least the first two moves."
⚠ Do not ask students to practise here. Practice happens in Sessions 1 and 2. Keep this to explanation and demo only.

5. Academic Vocabulary

Small changes make a big difference in how academic you sound:

Instead of "good"

  • Memorable — "...is more memorable." "A school trip is often more memorable than a textbook."
  • Worthwhile — "...was worthwhile." "Joining the drama club was worthwhile, even though it took a lot of time."
  • Rewarding — "...can be rewarding." "Teaching yourself a song on the guitar can be very rewarding."

Instead of "bad"

  • Time-consuming — "...can be time-consuming." "Learning a sport properly can be very time-consuming."
  • Frustrating — "It can be frustrating when..." "It can be frustrating when nobody is there to explain your mistake."
  • Monotonous — "...can feel monotonous." "Practising the same exercise every day can feel monotonous."

Instead of "I think":  "Speaking for myself, ..."  /  "What I have found is that..."

~4 mins Introduction
"One more tool before the discussions: Academic Vocabulary. The words you choose signal how carefully you are thinking. If you have joined earlier lessons, two of these — rewarding and monotonous — will be familiar, so they are a quick refresher. The other four are new, and they fit today's topic of learning outside the classroom well."
Walking through the vocabulary
"Instead of 'good,' try 'memorable' — a school trip is often more memorable than a textbook. Or 'worthwhile' — joining the drama club was worthwhile, even though it took a lot of time. Instead of 'bad,' try 'time-consuming' or 'frustrating' — it can be frustrating when nobody is there to explain your mistake. And instead of 'I think,' try 'Speaking for myself' or 'What I have found is that' — both sound natural when you answer from your own experience."
Read two or three aloud — you do not need all of them. Students pick up the rest through use in Sessions 1 and 2.
Transition into Session 1
"In Session 2, your challenge will be to use at least two of these words while still paraphrasing the question first. For now, let's move into the first discussion."

6. 1-on-1 Exchange — Session 1 (15 mins)

PROMPT

"Think of a skill you learned outside school lessons — at home, in a club, through a hobby, or on a trip. How was learning it different from learning in class?"

Steps

  • A: Share opinion using OREO
  • B: Ask one follow-up question
  • A: Respond → "How about you?"
  • B: Share opinion using OREO
  • A: Ask one follow-up question
  • Open discussion — agree, disagree, cultural differences
  • Tell your partner one thing they did well

Follow-up question ideas

  • "Who taught you — or did you teach yourself?"
  • "What was harder than learning in class?"
  • "How did you know you were improving?"
  • "Do students in your country usually learn that at school?"
Today's focus: When you answer the prompt — say the question back in your own words, then make it smaller. Follow-up questions are already small, so answer those directly.
When finished: Return to main room — instructor highlights 1-2 good paraphrases (1-2 mins)
15 mins Setup
"Into breakout rooms. Today's first prompt is on the screen. Before you answer it, do today's new move: say the question back in your own words, and choose one difference to focus on. Then give your OREO on that smaller version. Person B listens, asks one follow-up question, then it is your turn — and you paraphrase first too. Before you return, tell your partner one thing they did well. I'll move between rooms."
Any skill counts — cooking, a sport, an instrument, a game, a language app. The question is how the learning was different, so students do not need to be good at the skill.
Pairing
Pair students from different countries where possible — what students do outside school differs a lot between countries, and the contrast is the value. If numbers are uneven, the Assistant or Instructor steps in to keep pairs 1-on-1.
During breakout — what to listen for
✓ Does the speaker paraphrase at all, or jump straight into answering? Jumping in is the main failure mode.
✓ Are the key words changed, or is the question read back word for word?
✓ Is the paraphrase actually smaller than the original — one difference, not every difference?
⚠ If a student says "I do not do anything outside school," redirect: "That is fine — what have you learned at home, or from a video?"
After Session 1 — feedback (1-2 mins)
"Welcome back. I heard a good paraphrase: [quote it]. Notice the words changed — and how much easier the answer became once the question got smaller. Let's carry that into Session 2."
One or two observations only. Save detailed feedback for after Session 2.

7. 1-on-1 Exchange — Session 2 (15 mins)

PROMPT

"When you learn something in your free time, is it easier with friends or on your own? Why?"

Steps — same format as Session 1

  • A: Share opinion using OREO
  • B: Ask one follow-up question
  • A: Respond → "How about you?"
  • B: Share opinion using OREO
  • A: Ask one follow-up question
  • Open discussion — agree, disagree, cultural differences
  • Tell your partner one thing they did well

Session 2 Challenge

Use at least two of these words in your OREO response:

Memorable — "...is more memorable."
Worthwhile — "...was worthwhile."
Rewarding — "...can be rewarding."
Time-consuming — "...can be time-consuming."
Frustrating — "It can be frustrating when..."
Speaking for myself, ... — instead of "I think"
Today's focus: When you answer the prompt — keep paraphrasing first: say it back in new words, then make it smaller.
When finished: Return to main room — instructor feedback & group discussion (3 mins)
15 mins Setup
"Same steps, new prompt, one challenge. This time, try to use at least two of the Academic Vocabulary words on the right in your OREO response — do not force them, find a natural moment. And keep paraphrasing before you answer. Into breakout rooms."
During breakout — what to listen for
✓ Are students attempting the Academic Vocabulary? Even one word used naturally is worth highlighting.
✓ Are students still paraphrasing, or skipping straight to the answer now that the prompt feels easier? Note the strongest paraphrase for feedback.
⚠ If a student skips it, model it once: "Try starting with — so the question is whether I learn more easily by myself or with friends."
After Session 2 — group feedback (3 mins)
"Good work. First, a strong moment: [positive observation — e.g., 'someone said by easier, I mean less frustrating — defining the word made the whole answer sharper.']. Second, one thing to keep working on: [common issue — e.g., 'several paraphrases were the question read back word for word. Change at least the key words.']."
Experience corner — asking the Assistant / Instructor
"Assistant / Instructor — at university, what is one useful thing you learned outside your classes? How did you learn it?"
Keep it to one skill and one way of learning it. A short, specific answer is easy for students to relate to — and easy for them to ask about.

8. Reflection (8 mins)

Task 1 — Breakout Rooms (5 mins)

Room 1 Japanese students  |  Room 2 Indonesian students

In your own language, discuss:

"Which was harder today — changing the words of the question, or choosing one smaller part to answer? Why?"

Task 2 — Main Room (3 mins)

Share one reflection with everyone:

  • "Today I found it difficult to..."
  • "One paraphrasing phrase I want to use more is..."
  • "Something my partner said that surprised me was..."
8 mins Setup
"Last activity. Japanese students in Room 1, Indonesian students in Room 2. In your own language, discuss: which was harder today — changing the words of the question, or choosing one smaller part to answer? Why? The Assistant joins Room 1, the Instructor joins Room 2 to give one piece of advice each. Five minutes, then we share."
Brief for the Assistant / Instructor
Listen for what students find hard, and offer one practical tip each. For example: "If changing the words is hard, change just the main noun — 'young people' can become 'teenagers'. One changed word is a good start." / "If choosing a part is hard, pick the one where you already have a real example. That is always the right part to answer."
Task 2 — Main room (3 mins)
"Welcome back. Can one person from each group share — what was harder, and what advice did you get?"
Hear from 2-3 students. Do not go around the whole group — time is short.
Closing
"Great work today. Your answers got sharper as soon as the questions got smaller — that is exactly the technique. Next week is Logic & Writing, where we look at how to give feedback that actually helps someone improve their writing, using the Logic Map you complete for homework. See you then."

Logic Map for Next Week

Next week's class is Logic & Writing — we will write about learning outside the classroom, and then review each other's writing. To prepare, fill in the Logic Map Reference Sheet (shared as a PDF) for the prompt below.

PROMPT

"Schools should spend more time on activities outside the classroom, even if it means fewer lessons. To what extent do you agree or disagree?"

WHAT TO DO (required)
Complete the four parts of the map: Thesis, one Reason, one Example (keep it specific), and one Counter-argument with your response. Keep each line short and specific.
IF POSSIBLE (optional)
If you have time, turn your Logic Map into a short paragraph (about 80–100 words) using OREO. This step is optional — next week's class works well either way.

  Bring your completed map to next week's class — and your short paragraph too, if you wrote one.

~1 min Delivery
"Your homework for next week. Next class is Logic & Writing, where we turn today's thinking into written paragraphs. I've shared the Logic Map Reference Sheet as a PDF. On page 2, fill in a Logic Map for the prompt on screen — whether schools should spend more time on activities outside the classroom, even if it means fewer lessons. Completing the four parts is required. If you have extra time, you may also turn it into a short paragraph using OREO — that part is optional. Bring whatever you have."
Why the optional paragraph
Attempting a short paragraph at home — optionally — means next week's Writing class can focus on revising with a partner's feedback rather than drafting from zero. The Logic Map alone remains fully sufficient; the paragraph is a bonus for students who have time.
Connecting to today's skill
Remind them to use today's skill on the Thesis line: say the statement back in their own words, and decide what "activities outside the classroom" means — clubs, trips, volunteering? A Thesis that says what it means is far easier to support than one that tries to cover everything.
Closing
"That's it for today. Thank you — see you next week for Logic & Writing."
